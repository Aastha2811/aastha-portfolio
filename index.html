How I work

The habits behind the numbers, each with a real example from my work.

Finding the problem

Size the leak in data, then go find the why from people.

  • Start where the numbers drop: which step, which cohort, how big.
  • Ask one question across user calls, in-person conversations and support tickets.
  • Write the problem as a moment in the user's journey, not as a feature request.
In practice

Data showed only ~31% of customers ever reorder. Fifty conversations showed why: everyone judges results at week 2–3, so I built the journey around that moment.

Read the case →
Prioritising

Few bets, each tied to one metric, smallest version first.

  • Rank by impact on the metric that matters against effort; low-effort, high-impact work goes first.
  • Commit to a short list with a success measure for each, and say no to the rest out loud.
  • Keep v1 simple and configurable, so iterating doesn't need a new release.
In practice

Mapped seven problems across the post-purchase journey, then committed only two to leadership: Day 0–7 onboarding and a week 2–3 results check, each with its own metric.

Read the case →
Design decisions

Prototype the real thing, let behaviour pick the winner.

  • Share clickable mockups, not static screens, so feedback is about the actual interaction.
  • Test the variants that matter: visibility, context, placement.
  • When results tie, choose what holds up as a permanent part of the product, and design for the most common screen.
In practice

Tested five versions of a UV widget over two rounds. Two placements tied, so I shipped the one beside the offers, where people decide to buy.

Read the case →
Managing stakeholders

Show, don't describe, and lead with the outcome.

  • Open docs with the business result, not the mechanics behind it.
  • Flag risks early, with evidence: legal claims, broken copy, device-level error data.
  • Say what the data doesn't prove, so trust survives the next result.
In practice

Brought leadership, brand and design to one direction for a launch page within a day, and went from concept to live in about 12 days.

Turning first-time buyers into repeat customers

Most first-time buyers of our Glow Vitamin C serum (GVC) never ordered again. I went out to find out why, then built a guided journey around the moment people decide whether a product works.

2×+

repeat rate for journey completers vs other GVC buyers (23% vs 10%)

9% → 17%

Day 0 scan completion after magic-link auto-login

23% vs 10%

repeat among first-time app buyers, the leak we set out to fix

Project
GVC Retention Journey
Role
Product Manager, research to rollout
Shipped
V1 app 19 Jul · V2 AI scan 26 Aug · web
Research
~20 user calls, 30 in-person interviews, CRM tickets
Tools
BigQuery, Mixpanel, VWO, WhatsApp

The problem

  • About 2 in 3 customers don't place a second order within a year. Only ~31% ever repeat, so the first-to-second order step is the single biggest leak.
  • Retention is eroding as acquisition scales: ~35% for 2022–23 cohorts, ~27–28% for 2025 cohorts.
  • GVC is our #2 acquisition serum but the weakest retainer of any serum, with 13.5% 90-day repeat and the lowest LTV.
  • 54.8% of GVC buyers are new to Foxtale, and many are new to Vitamin C. The bottle runs out around Day 55–60, and the second-order window closes by ~Day 90.

Finding the why

The data showed people weren't coming back, but not why. So I asked one question across user calls, conversations near stores and malls in Mumbai, and a read of post-purchase CRM tickets: what happens between the first order and the reorder decision?

Key insight

Every user reaches a results judgement at week 2–3. That moment decides the reorder.

Good change

Trust forms and a reorder becomes likely.

Bad change

The product is dropped for good.

No change

The biggest silent loss. No signal ever reaches the brand.

Seven problems, mapped

StageProblemSignal
Pre-usageDelivery uncertainty: WISMO ~36%, cancellations ~21%Medium
Day 0–3Users work out usage themselvesStrong
Day 0–7Users focus on avoiding reactions, not on getting resultsStrong
Week 2–3No visible change, so users drop silentlyStrong
Week 3–6Every reorder is a fresh decisionStrong
Day 0–60No brand presence at key momentsMedium
Whole journeyBeginners have no success criteria or timelineEarly

I committed two interventions to leadership on 29 April: guided onboarding for Day 0–7, measured by week-1 active users, and a results checkpoint for week 2–3, measured by week-2 to week-4 continuation.

What we built

The existing WhatsApp flow was a message tree with no reason to come back. I stress-tested it against the Hooked model and rebuilt it as Skin Streak and Progress Mirror, then anchored it on GVC and the user's own skin.

  • Day 21 is the before/after scan: glow and hydration are visible by then, it's the habit sweet spot, and pigmentation is set as a 6+ week expectation instead of over-promised.
  • Day 42 is the refill nudge, timed before the bottle runs out.
  • WhatsApp was capped at two anchor moments to control cost.

Funnel fixes that moved numbers

Magic-link auto-login, live 29 Sep

The post-delivery WhatsApp link dropped users onto a login wall. Once people tapped Start, completion was ~60% either way, so the whole gain came from removing the login.

A "21-day challenge" variant lost to Control, 20% started vs 28%, a statistically significant gap. Its "Remind me later" option gave people an exit, and only 2 of 26 came back. Control shipped.

  • V1 held up over a longer window too: 30% of completers bought again within 60 days, vs 19% of all GVC buyers.
  • The lift holds on app and web, for new and repeat customers, and is strongest among first-time buyers.
  • Most of it comes from other products. GVC rebuys move less, which fits a 30-day window that ends before the bottle runs out.

What's next

  • Scale to moisturisers, starting with the Brightening Moisturizer, then extend to NS12 buyers.
  • Move every post-delivery message to the magic link, roughly +35 scans a week.
  • Set up a holdout group for a causal read.

Caveats

  • Completers opted in, so they may have been more engaged to begin with. This shows correlation, not proven cause.
  • A holdout group is needed to prove the journey caused the lift.

Making an AI skin scan worth coming back to

Our AI skin scan was a one-time novelty: most people viewed their report once and never came back. I turned it into a 30-day rescan journey that gives people a reason to return and proof their routine works.

87.5%

of August report viewers opened it more than once

Project
Skin Horoscope 2.0
Role

Live

Built on

Tools

Where it started

V1 launched in September 2025: a short quiz, a face scan, then a skin report with product recommendations. It became the main source of skin-type data for app users, but nothing gave anyone a reason to rescan.

  • 73% of users viewed their report only once; only 27% ever came back.
  • Every scan used paid third-party credits, and cost scaled with usage on technology we didn't own.
Build vs buy

Replacing the vendor with a proprietary pipeline: a conditional yes. Run it in shadow mode first, A/B test before rollout, and secure IP ownership in the contract.

What we built

The core idea: turn a one-time scan into a 30-day rescan journey. Success means people complete the 30 days and scan again.

Day 0 · reminder opt-in→Days 1–29 · personalised card→Day 30 · card unlocks→Second scan
"Your cart's waiting"

No purchase yet since the first scan.

"Glow-up mode ON"

One or two orders.

"You're consistent"

Three or more orders.

  • Kept v1 simple: purchase state counts only completed orders after the first scan, with no category filters or add-to-cart counting.
  • Made everything configurable in Strapi CMS, so copy and cards change without an app release.
  • Led the stakeholder doc with retention outcomes, not CMS mechanics.

What moved

Report views per userBeforeAfter 2.0
Viewed only once73%38%
Returned more than once27%62%
Returned 2 times16%44%
Returned 3 times5%15%

The reminder opt-in was new: 14% of report viewers (1,379 of 9,730) asked to be reminded. When the raw averages were challenged, I re-cut the data as a share of users, and the shift held.

Scan-to-purchase within 7 days climbed from 11–12% in April and May to 16% by August and September.

Problems caught and fixed

A face-detection error spike

Errors jumped from ~2% to ~14% while traffic stayed flat, which ruled out sale traffic. I traced it to Android 13 and older on Chinese OEM phones (Xiaomi 24%, OPPO 16%, realme/OnePlus 17% vs Apple 3%), saw retries per user nearly double, and escalated to engineering with device-level evidence.

A web-to-app bridge

A strip on the Vitamin C Serum page offers a free AI skin scan in the app. I chose "AI scan" over "horoscope" so the promise matches what people find after installing, and placed it after Expected Results so it doesn't compete with Add to Cart.

The next problem: intent without purchase

Engagement and intent are strong: 38.8% of report viewers add to cart. But only 25.1% of those carts become orders, and just 6.6% of users come back after 24 hours.

  • Now in motion with a teammate I lead: horoscope-specific abandoned-cart push and WhatsApp messages tied to each user's own report.
  • A journey for scanners who never buy, and a redesign of the onboarding screens.

Caveats

  • Scanners opt in and may be higher-intent to begin with, so the purchase lift isn't proven cause.
  • After the first month scanners buy less than average app users (~5% vs ~7% a month), so the effect fades without a reason to return.
  • Scan conversion is reported on a 7-day basis throughout; earlier reads used other windows and aren't comparable.

An AI scan that picks the right sunscreen

An AI face scan that recommends the right sunscreen, launched as part of a wider sunscreen push. It tripled scan participation, and then I had to keep its scan costs from running away.

Launched

Tools
Mixpanel, GlamAR

What it is

A guided flow that ends in the cart, with several entry points across the app leading into the scan. Entry points went in once the second version of the AI scan was signed off.

A few questions→AI face scan→Sunscreen pick→Product page→Cart

What moved

Monthly scansNormal monthSale month
Before (skin report scan only)22,35548,771
With the sunscreen scan live73,115173,100
of which sunscreen scan15,00087,900
Growth+227%+255%
  • Skin report scans grew too, so the jump wasn't only from adding a new scan type.
  • The sunscreen scan converted better: 3.99% vs 2.16% from onboarding to purchase in April 2026.
  • Scan participation settled at around 30–35%.

Problems caught and fixed

Scan credits burning too fast

The sunscreen scan used ~34% of all paid scan credits, with a far bigger sale-month surge than the skin report scan. Credits ran out in April and scan errors rose. I built a scan review dashboard and five Mixpanel alerts for credit burn and error spikes, flagged scans by logged-out users as wasted credits, and forecast that the remaining credits would last through August with ~28K scans to spare.

Scans were sale-driven, not a habit

The daily scan rate fell 58% after the sale, and normal-month scans were declining ~19% month on month. That shaped what came next.

What's next

  • Move the scan to a more visible spot and extend it into seasonal recommendations on other product pages.
  • A sunscreen retention journey built around a daily-use habit rather than visible results.
  • A sunscreen scan funnel in the retention MIS, to check whether scans turn into orders or only into credit cost.

Caveats

  • The 3.99% vs 2.16% comparison is between two different funnels and audiences, so treat it as directional.
  • ~85% of sunscreen scans happen in sale months, which inflates both volume and conversion.

Sources: Mixpanel, scan review dashboard, vendor credit records, Q1–Q2 roadmap.

Using live UV levels to sell more sunscreen

Showing shoppers their live local UV Index on the sunscreen product page, so sun protection feels relevant right now. Two rounds of testing decided what it should look like and where it should sit.

+29.7%

conversion lift for the shipped variant vs control (1.18% → 1.53%)

1.40% → 1.65%

no UV info vs a sticky UV widget in round 1

2 rounds

and 5 variants tested before locking design and placement

Project
UV Index widget
Role
Product Manager
Priority
P0 · low effort, high expected impact
Live
Web by 26 Feb; Tinted sunscreen on app + web from 1 Apr
Campaign
"Play On Till the Sun's Gone"

What we built

A widget on the sunscreen PDP showing the shopper's local UV level. When the current reading was low or moderate, it showed the day's peak instead, so the message stayed relevant. It went live first on the three core sunscreens: Matte, Dewy and Glow.

UV Index · Peak today
8Very high
LowModerateHighExtreme

Illustration of the shipped widget with its severity scale.

Round 1: does visibility matter?

VariantWhat users sawConversion
ControlNo UV info1.40%
Variant 1UV widget, not sticky, with peak UV1.49%
Variant 2 · winnerSticky widget at the top, with peak UV1.65%

Adding the widget alone barely moved conversion. Making it sticky did: visibility and persistence are what make it work.

Round 2: a severity scale, and where it sits

VariantWhat users sawConversion
ControlWidget at the top, no scale1.18%
Variant 1Widget at the top, with scale1.55%
Variant 2 · shippedWidget above offers, with scale1.53%

The scale drove the lift. The two placements performed almost identically, so I shipped the one above offers: it puts the UV message right where purchase intent peaks, and works as a permanent slot without crowding the top of the page.

What I learned

  • A useful signal only works if people see it: sticky beat non-sticky.
  • A number needs context. The severity scale turned "UV 8" into something shoppers understood.
  • Placement near offers ties the message to the buying moment.
  • When two variants tie, pick the one that holds up as a permanent part of the page.

Caveats

  • The two rounds ran at different times with different controls, so compare variants within a round, not across rounds.
  • Sample sizes and significance weren't recorded in the pre-reads.

Sources: website pre-reads (12 and 26 Feb 2026), Q1 roadmap, Tinted sunscreen rollout.

Pre-orders for a product with no reviews yet

An explorable salon scene as the early-access page for the Brightening Moisturizer, a brand-new product with no reviews yet. From concept to live page in about twelve days.

The brief

Launch "India's 1st moisturizer that works like a daily facial" with an early-access page that builds anticipation and captures intent before sale. The catch: a brand-new product with no reviews, where a standard landing page wouldn't stand out.

The concept

One explorable scene of the Foxtale pink salon, extending the campaign's visual world, with eight hotspots that each reveal part of the product story. I shared it as a working, clickable mockup so leadership could react to the real interaction, not a static design.

How it evolved

  • Visual direction: leadership wanted a top-down "Barbie salon" with the brand mascot for cheekiness; the brand team asked for chrome over brass and gold to match the PR kit. Aligned in a day.
  • Sign-up to pre-order: a pre-order page should be conversion first, so the CTA became a simple "Pre-Order Now" with the freebie as a small callout on a crowded mobile layout.
  • Copy and claims: wrote copy requirements across 9 surfaces, flagged a desktop/mobile CTA mismatch and claims needing legal sign-off, and kept glow and hydration claims separate.
  • Live, then iterated: hotspots weren't prominent enough, so I changed their effect and size, tuned tap targets for 390px, and led the ingredients with Vitamin C, Papaya Enzyme and 5-Ceramide Complex.
  • Said no to a clickable thumbnail on the bottom sheet: an image popup over a sheet feels broken on mobile.

Results, launch week

MetricResult

Pre-order rate, non-explorers21%
Average time on page26–29 sec
Share of hotspot clicks on The Product40%
Top 3 hotspots' share of clicks68%
Opened only one hotspot58%
Power users, 5+ hotspots14%

People want product info first, then "is this for me", then urgency. And there's a cliff after the first hotspot: most people open one and leave.

For the next launch

  • Design for the middle: put a second hook inside the first hotspot.
  • The product is always the hero hotspot; make it the most prominent entry point.
  • Urgency and personalisation close the sale; they don't open it.
  • Track interaction depth, not just time on page, and don't start A/B tests during launch traffic.
  • Pre-order works for a product with no reviews. It's worth turning into a playbook.

Caveats

  • Explorers chose to engage, so the 33% vs 21% gap shows correlation, not proven cause.
  • All results come from launch week, when intent is unusually high.
